\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) augmented with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) suffer severe performance degradation when addressing multi-hop relational queries that require traversing complex causal chains across scientific and biomedical knowledge graphs. Classical GraphRAG architectures rely on Markovian random walks (e.g., Personalized PageRank) or community clustering, which suffer from diffusive probability dilution ($\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{t})$) and entrapment in high-degree hub nodes. In this paper, we propose \textbf{Q-GraphRAG}, a hybrid quantum-classical retrieval architecture that leverages \textbf{Continuous-Time Quantum Walks (CTQW)} over graph-derived Heisenberg Hamiltonians to accelerate multi-hop topological exploration. By exploiting quantum superposition and constructive interference in a $2^N$-dimensional Hilbert space, Q-GraphRAG achieves ballistic wavepacket propagation ($\mathcal{O}(t)$) across multi-hop reasoning chains. We evaluate Q-GraphRAG across a 50-item multi-domain benchmark encompassing Clinical Oncology (PubMedQA), Precision Medicine (PrimeKG), Antimicrobial Resistance (ChEMBL), and Quantum Condensed Matter Physics. Q-GraphRAG achieves an average \textbf{+11.8\% improvement in Retrieval Precision @ 5} ($p < 0.01$) and a \textbf{+57.7\% increase in multi-hop Answer F1} over pure dense vector baselines. Furthermore, we validate hardware feasibility by compiling and transpiling our graph Hamiltonian evolution circuits into native OpenQASM 2.0 specifications on IBM Quantum Heavy-Hex superconducting QPU topologies, demonstrating an experimental state fidelity of $\mathcal{F} = 0.9926$.

\keywords{Quantum GraphRAG \and Continuous-Time Quantum Walk \and Knowledge Graphs \and Multi-Hop Reasoning \and Precision Medicine \and NISQ Hardware.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The application of advanced computational models---which include both Large Language Models (LLMs) and deep neural networks---can substantially enhance the process of automated knowledge extraction and assist with biomedical decision-making \cite{lewis2020retrieval}. Unfortunately, there is a high rate of diagnostic errors and factual hallucination associated with the exclusive reliance on parametric memory when using LLMs to analyze complex biological mechanisms of diseases, such as oncological signaling pathways. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) provides a principled mechanism to mitigate hallucinations by dynamically retrieving contextually relevant passages from external repositories prior to answer synthesis \cite{lewis2020retrieval,edge2024graphrag}.

Although RAG has demonstrated widespread success in single-hop semantic look-up tasks, the traditional paradigm of dense vector retrieval (i.e., cosine similarity over dense neural embeddings) exhibits fundamental drawbacks in multi-hop relational reasoning. For example, in complex biomedical domains characterized by interconnected oncological signaling cascades (e.g., chromosomal translocations $\rightarrow$ chimeric kinase formation $\rightarrow$ downstream pathway activation $\rightarrow$ targeted drug inhibition), the appropriate clinical response cannot be localized within a single document chunk. Rather, critical causal dependencies are distributed across disparate topological nodes in a Knowledge Graph (KG).

To overcome these structural limitations, Graph-Augmented RAG (GraphRAG) systems integrate structured Knowledge Graphs with dense vector stores. However, the majority of current state-of-the-art classical methods for graph retrieval---such as Personalized PageRank (PPR) and Breadth-First Search (BFS) expansions---rely on classical diffusion processes. Classical diffusion processes are inherently Markovian and diffusive: the probability amplitude associated with reaching an $H$-hop target node decays exponentially with distance ($\mathcal{O}(1/H)$), while probability mass becomes excessively trapped in high-degree hub entities (e.g., generic biomedical terms such as ``protein'' or ``cell'').

This paper introduces \textbf{Q-GraphRAG}, a novel hybrid quantum-classical retrieval architecture that utilizes unitary \textbf{Continuous-Time Quantum Walks (CTQW)} \cite{childs2002continuous,childs2003exponential} in place of classical random-walk diffusion. By mapping candidate multi-hop subgraphs into parameterized Heisenberg spin Hamiltonians, Q-GraphRAG exploits quantum state superposition and constructive interference of wavepackets to facilitate ballistic wavepacket propagation across the diameter of the graph ($\mathcal{O}(t)$). Consequently, it enables the discovery of deep entity relationships beyond the reach of classical random walk methods without suffering from hub-node entrapment.

\subsection{Key Contributions}
The primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quantum Retrieval Architecture:} We formulate a hybrid retrieval framework that leverages Continuous-Time Quantum Walks over graph-derived Heisenberg Hamiltonians to accelerate topologically directed exploration in Knowledge Graphs.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Multi-Domain Benchmark ($N=50$):} We evaluate Q-GraphRAG across a curated 50-item benchmark spanning Clinical Oncology (PubMedQA), Precision Medicine (PrimeKG), Antimicrobial Resistance (ChEMBL), and Quantum Condensed Matter Physics. Q-GraphRAG achieves a \textbf{+11.8\% improvement in Retrieval Precision @ 5} ($p < 0.01$) and a \textbf{+57.7\% increase in multi-hop Answer F1} compared to pure dense vector baselines.
    \item \textbf{Physical NISQ Validation:} We compile and transpile our graph Hamiltonian evolution circuits into native OpenQASM 2.0 specifications on IBM Quantum Heavy-Hex superconducting QPU topologies, demonstrating an experimental state fidelity of $\mathcal{F} = 0.9926$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Graph-Augmented RAG and Classical Restrictions}
Recent RAG research has attempted to address the restrictions of dense embedding representations using structured knowledge graphs. Zhang et al. (2026) \cite{zhang2026hybrid} proposed hybrid dense-sparse vector retrieval, but they discarded the graph structure to remove causal chain connections between different document segments. Edge et al. (2025) \cite{edge2025modular} introduced Microsoft's GraphRAG as a way to retain graph topology and apply Leiden clustering for global summarization. Although GraphRAG provides a useful overview at a macro level, it produces a hierarchical summary of entities that fails to provide detailed information about local relationships, including very specific drug-gene interactions. Also, Li and Chen (2026) \cite{li2026hierarchical} used multi-level graph clustering to reduce summarization latency, but they also lost contextual information regarding relational semantics. 

There are ways to improve upon iterative neighborhood expansion for expanding an entity's neighborhood through a beam search method presented by Sun et al. (2026) \cite{sun2026iterative}; however, these methods will result in exponentially increasing computational complexity in terms of hops ($\mathcal{O}(d^H)$), thereby making 3-hop and 4-hop traversals computationally prohibitive for production environments. Finally, Kwon et al. (2026) \cite{kwon2026adaptive} used GNNs for selecting subgraphs, but their methodology relies on traditional message-passing techniques, where messages decay with increasing distance from the edge and fail to account for constructive wave interference. A key issue is that Venkatesh et al. (2025) \cite{venkatesh2025topological} found that when employing classical Markovian diffusion-based algorithms (Personalized PageRank) to biomedical KGs, the PPR algorithm suffers from significant ``hub-trapping,'' where a large amount of probability mass becomes concentrated in high-degree nodes (e.g., ``cells'', ``proteins'') and suppresses the detection of low-frequency target genes. As a result, the probability amplitude decays exponentially with distance ($\mathcal{O}(1/H)$).

\subsection{Continuous-Time Quantum Walks and Wavepacket Dynamics}
In order to overcome the limitations of classical Markovian diffusion processes, QML researchers have been studying the application of quantum graph algorithms. Schuld et al. (2026) \cite{schuld2026continuous} provided mathematical proof that Continuous-Time Quantum Walks (CTQWs) provide a quadratic improvement in walk propagation ($\mathcal{O}(t)$) compared to the classical $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{t})$. In addition to this, Grover and Sengupta (2025) \cite{grover2025quantum} showed that quantum wavepackets travel through high-degree nodes without experiencing a loss in velocity due to constructive interference and therefore do not experience hub-trapping as observed with classical PageRank. 

Wong and Meyer (2025) \cite{wong2025discrete} demonstrated that CTQW outperforms DTQW for all types of graph mining tasks since CTQW can map directly to physical Hamiltonian time-evolution ($e^{-iHt}$) without introducing an artificial coin space. The previous studies that applied CTQW---e.g., the spatial search problem on irregular networks by Chakraborty et al. (2026) \cite{chakraborty2026quantum} and the Heisenberg Hamiltonian mapping by Biamonte and Morales (2026) \cite{biamonte2026hamiltonian}---were either highly theoretical or based on completely synthetic random graphs (e.g., Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi). This study represents the first integration of CTQW into an LLM/RAG framework and a real-world textual knowledge base.

\subsection{Quantum Graph Kernels and Hybrid QML}
A major contribution in hybrid QML was made by Huang et al. (2026) \cite{huang2026quantum}. They provide an analytical proof that the inner product in Hilbert space can be used for the purpose of graph matching. Their findings indicate that quantum models are able to predict properties of graphs with exponentially fewer physical measurements than classical algorithms do. However, the work done by Park and Kim (2025) \cite{park2025hilbert} that assesses the similarity of molecular graphs using fidelity kernels $K(G_1,G_2)=|\langle\psi(G_1)|\psi(G_2)\rangle|^2$, has limitations on the size of the molecules they evaluate ($N \le 8$). They also do not consider how to prune large-scale KGs. 

To reduce the time-consuming optimization loops of PQCs described by Verdon et al. (2025) \cite{verdon2025quantum}, one should prefer parameter-free physical Hamiltonian time evolution. Moreover, Cerezo et al. (2026) \cite{cerezo2026barren} have shown that shallow Trotter-Suzuki steps (with $m \le 3$) will effectively prevent both barren plateaus and vanishing gradients in quantum graph circuits. Regarding the computation side, Broughton et al. (2025) \cite{broughton2025tensor} analyze the CPU/GPU statevector simulation performance bottlenecks. Therefore, based on their analysis it has been established that if we want to bound the subgraphs to $N \le 14$ qubits, then this is the optimal threshold for sub-50ms CPU/GPU executions before $\mathcal{O}(2^N)$ memory limits induce significant latency.

\subsection{Biomedical Benchmarks and Research Gap}
The clinical rationale for multi-hop quantum retrieval is supported by Jin et al. (2026) \cite{jin2026biomedical}, which demonstrates that traditional dense vector RAG models fail to solve 68\% of multi-hop medical questions (such as those found in PubMedQA) since there are necessary facts distributed over disjointed text passages. Furthermore, Chandak et al. (2026) \cite{chandak2026precision} support the necessity of traversing 3-hop to 5-hop causal pathways in precision medicine through developing PrimeKG. For systematically measuring retrieval efficiency, Es et al. (2025) \cite{es2025automated} developed the RAGAS framework and provided common evaluation axes, e.g., Faithfulness and Answer F1.

Despite individual advances in each area, a critical research gap exists: classical GraphRAG is bottlenecked by hub-trapping and exponential distance decay ($\mathcal{O}(1/H)$), while CTQW and QML research remains either theoretical or is mostly restricted to small synthetic molecule benchmarks. \textbf{Q-GraphRAG} fills this gap as the first cloud-native architecture combining continuous-time quantum walks, topological pruning (bounded by $N \le 14$), asynchronous task orchestration, and LLMs to perform real-world multi-hop biomedical retrievals.
